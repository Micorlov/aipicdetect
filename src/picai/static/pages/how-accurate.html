— Accuracy

How accurate is an AI image detector?

Less than the marketing suggests, and picai is no exception. This page explains what the score means, where the model is likely to be wrong, and how to use the result responsibly.

The score is a probability

The detector is an image classifier trained on pictures labelled "generated" or "real". For a new picture it outputs how strongly the pixels resemble each class. picai reports that as an AI likelihood from 0 to 100 percent, labels anything near the middle Uncertain, and attaches a confidence band. It never says "this is fake". A 90 percent score means the pixels look a lot like the generated images the model has seen, not that a court would agree.

What the benchmarks say

Independent evaluations paint a consistent picture: detectors do well on the generators they were trained against and degrade on newer ones. A 2025 benchmark of sixteen public detectors found average accuracy around three quarters on images from 2020 and 2021 generators, falling to well under half on 2024 models, and to a coin flip or worse on the latest commercial generators such as Flux and recent Midjourney versions. Commercial detectors that retrain continuously report much higher numbers on their own test sets; those numbers rarely transfer to images found in the wild.

picai ships with an open-source model, haywoodsloan/ai-image-detector-deploy, chosen because it runs on a CPU and can be inspected. Expect it to be strongest on Stable Diffusion-era images and weakest on the newest generators. If a better open model appears, point PICAI_DETECTOR_MODEL at it: see self-hosting.

What makes results worse

  • Re-encoding. Screenshots, messaging apps, social media uploads and heavy JPEG compression all erase the fine-grained patterns detectors rely on.
  • Resizing and cropping. Small thumbnails carry little signal; tight crops remove context.
  • Post-processing. Filters, grain, upscaling and editing an AI image in a photo editor push it toward "real"; heavy retouching of a real photo can push it toward "AI".
  • Unusual subjects. Illustrations, renders, scans and medical or scientific imagery sit outside the training distribution.

How to read a result

  1. Look at the confidence band before the number. Low or Uncertain means the model is guessing.
  2. Check the Metadata card. A C2PA manifest naming a generator, or a camera EXIF block with a plausible lens and exposure, is often more informative than the score.
  3. Get a second opinion. Run the picture through another detector, and do a reverse image search to find the earliest copy.
  4. Never act on a single score. Treat it as one signal among several, especially when the consequence is accusing someone of fabrication.

Detectors are an arms race. A score that is reliable today can be meaningless after the next generator release. That is a reason to prefer open tools whose model you can see and replace, not a reason to trust any of them blindly.
